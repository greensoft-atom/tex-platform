5. Contract and proposal rules
   A. The commercial negotiation workflow
   The platform should store each proposal as an immutable version. Parties may negotiate the following:

- Deal type and licensed or transferred assets.
- Scope, territory, duration, exclusivity and permitted use.
- Deliverables, milestones and acceptance tests.
- Price, currency, taxes, payment dates and royalties.
- Background IP and ownership of new results.
- Confidentiality, permitted disclosure and data handling.
- Warranties, liability, indemnities and remedies.
- Termination, refunds, outstanding payments and dispute resolution.
- Required regulatory approvals, registrations and conditions precedent.
  Example:

1. The provider proposes a non-exclusive license for RMB 300,000.
2. The buyer counters with RMB 200,000 upfront and RMB 100,000 after a technical milestone.
3. The provider accepts the payment split but changes the licensed territory.
4. The buyer must explicitly accept the new complete proposal version.
5. The final term sheet is generated and the parties review the contract.
6. The authorized representatives execute the definitive agreement.
7. The platform checks whether any regulatory or other closing conditions remain.
   The RMB figures are illustrative only. The platform should not assume that agreeing commercial terms alone makes every technology-transfer contract effective or immediately performable.
   B. Contract records and signature evidence
   For each contract, preserve:

- Contract ID, type, language and governing-law/jurisdiction fields.
- Exact version and cryptographic document hash.
- Parties and the legal entities they represent.
- Signatory identity, role and recorded authority.
- Signature/seal evidence and timestamps.
- Effective date and conditions precedent.
- Attachments, schedules, amendments and superseded versions.
- Required regulatory approvals, registrations and certificates.
- The relationship between clauses, obligations, milestones and payment instructions.
  China's Electronic Signature Law provides that qualifying reliable electronic signatures have the same legal effect as handwritten signatures or seals. C. Legal gate for cross-border technology deals
A cross-border deal should have a separate review workflow. Before the system permits controlled disclosure, contract execution or delivery, it should determine:

1. Where the provider, recipient and intended user are located.
2. Whether the technology falls into a prohibited, restricted or freely transferable category.
3. Whether an export/import license, prior approval, contract registration or other filing is required.
4. Whether the transaction involves controlled technical data, dual-use items, sensitive information or other regulated content.
5. Whether the payment and settlement path complies with applicable currency, banking, tax and foreign-exchange rules.
6. Whether personal information or important data will cross borders and, if so, what legal mechanism is required.
7. Whether the contract and transfer are permitted under the applicable IP rights, ownership and existing agreements.

B. Payment statuses
Use separate status fields for the obligation, payment, funds availability, release instruction and payout.
Object Suggested statuses
Payment obligation draft, approved, due, partially_satisfied, satisfied, cancelled, disputed
Payment request created, sent, pending, expired, cancelled, failed
Provider transaction initiated, processing, succeeded, failed, reversed, refunded, partially_refunded
Provider-held funds, if the product supports it not_applicable, pending, confirmed, reserved, partially_released, released, frozen, returned
Release instruction not_eligible, awaiting_approval, approved, submitted, provider_pending, completed, failed, cancelled
Payout not_requested, requested, pending, paid, failed, returned, reversed
Do not make these statuses interchangeable. For example, a successful payment transaction may not mean funds are available for payout, and a submitted release instruction does not mean the recipient has been paid.
C. Funding calculation
For each obligation, store at least:

- Gross amount due.
- Currency.
- Amount received and provider-confirmed.
- Amount refunded or reversed.
- Amount reserved or otherwise restricted, if supported.
- Amount released/transferred.
- Platform fee and payment-provider fee.
- Tax or withholding amounts, where applicable.
- Outstanding amount.
- Provider transaction IDs and reconciliation references.
  A simplified outstanding amount is:
  \[
  \text{Outstanding}
  =
  \text{Gross obligation}
- \text{Confirmed qualifying payments}

* \text{Applicable reversals}

- \text{Valid credits}
  \]

This is an accounting illustration, not a complete legal or tax calculation. Each currency must be reconciled independently, and FX conversion must have a recorded rate, source and timestamp.
D. When can funds be released?
A release should require all of the following:

1. The payment obligation is linked to an executed contract or otherwise valid arrangement.
2. The relevant legal and compliance gates are satisfied.
3. The required funds are confirmed and eligible under the payment partner's product.
4. The contractual release condition is met.
5. Required technical acceptance or other evidence is present.
6. The approving actor has authority for the transaction.
7. There is no active hold or dispute that contractually or legally blocks release.
8. The release amount does not exceed the eligible amount.
9. The request is idempotent and has a unique provider reference.
10. The provider confirms the result and the ledger is reconciled.
    Do not implement a platform admin override that directly releases money. If an exceptional release is legally allowed, it should use a separately authorized, audited exception workflow with the payment institution.
    E. Royalties and recurring payments
    Licensing and commercialization deals may have obligations long after the initial transfer. The platform should support:

- Royalty reporting periods and due dates.
- Sales or usage reports from the licensee.
- Minimum royalties and agreed thresholds.
- Currency and tax fields.
- Report review and correction.
- Payment request generation.
- Late payment reminders and disputes.
- Contract amendments and renewal dates.
- Audit rights where the contract provides them.
  A deal can be closed for initial delivery while the royalty schedule remains active. Therefore, deal_closed must not automatically cancel future contractual obligations.

7. Fee model for the exchange
   The platform should support a configurable fee schedule, but the exact fees are a commercial decision rather than something established by technology-transfer law.
   Recommended fee policy
   Fee Recommended rule Trigger
   Account / organization registration Free at launch None
   Listing fee Free at launch to build supply and demand Publication, only if later introduced
   Managed-service fee Fixed or quoted based on work scope When the client accepts the service order or applicable contract milestone
   Success fee Contractually defined fixed fee or percentage Explicit qualifying event, such as completion of a defined transaction
   Technical assessment Quoted fee for an expert engagement Written approval of the scope and quote
   Legal/IP service Separate professional-service fee Accepted engagement terms
   Payment-provider fee Pass through or allocate according to disclosed terms Provider transaction
   Refund/reversal cost Allocate only under disclosed terms and applicable law Actual refund/reversal event
   Ongoing royalty administration Optional fee if the platform provides continuing reporting/collection services Contractually defined reporting period or service milestone
   Required fee rules
   Every fee definition must include:

- Fee payer and recipient.
- Calculation basis and rate or fixed amount.
- Currency and tax treatment.
- The exact event that triggers the fee.
- Whether it is deducted from the transaction or invoiced separately.
- Refundability and cancellation treatment.
- Treatment of partial payment, chargeback, reversal and failed payout.
- Whether a fee is calculated before or after taxes and payment-provider charges.
  For example, if the parties agree a success fee based on the amount actually received by the provider, the system must use the defined eligible amount—not the listing's asking price or the proposed contract value.
  Never deduct a fee simply because an introduction was accepted unless that is an expressly agreed paid service.

8. Disputes, rejection and refunds
   A managed exchange needs a structured dispute process, but it must not promise that the platform can decide every legal dispute or reverse every payment.
   Dispute categories

- Technical deliverable does not meet the agreed specification.
- A milestone is late or incomplete.
- IP ownership, license scope or authority is disputed.
- Confidential information was disclosed or used outside the permitted scope.
- Payment is missing, late, duplicated or incorrectly allocated.
- A refund or payout has failed.
- A party alleges misrepresentation, fraud or non-performance.
- A cross-border transfer or required approval is blocked.
- The parties disagree about royalty calculations or ongoing obligations.

Rules for disputed funds

1. Record the exact obligation and amount being contested.
2. Apply only holds that the contract, provider arrangement and applicable law permit.
3. Preserve relevant evidence and notify the parties.
4. Prevent the same disputed amount from being released twice.
5. Keep undisputed obligations separate where legally and operationally possible.
6. Record the outcome and its authority.
7. Issue refund/release instructions only through the approved payment mechanism.
8. Reconcile the provider result before closing the financial case.
   A dispute flag in your database does not itself freeze money at a bank or payment provider. The provider must support the relevant hold, and the contractual and legal basis must exist.
   The platform's dispute service should be described as case management, facilitated resolution or mediation, depending on the service actually provided. Do not represent it as a court or binding arbitral tribunal unless you have established the appropriate legal framework.

9. Required database and backend modules
   The existing technology, demand, organization, introduction and deal records remain useful. The final design should add or formalize the following modules.
   Module Core records Main purpose

   Technology and IP technologies, ip_assets, ownership_evidence, technology_offers, technology_versions What is offered, who claims rights, and what evidence supports those claims
   Discovery demands, matches, shortlists, introductions Buyer needs, candidates and consent to introductions
   Negotiation deal_proposals, proposal_versions, proposal_responses, approval_requests Versioned offers, counteroffers and authorized decisions

   Compliance compliance_cases, technology_control_reviews, regulatory_documents, cross_border_reviews Required legal and regulatory gates

   Payment obligations payment_obligations, invoices, fee_assessments Contractual amounts due and platform charges
   Provider integration payment_transactions, provider_events, funding_allocations, release_instructions, payouts, refunds External payment operations and verified outcomes
   Accounting ledger_accounts, ledger_entries, reconciliations Auditable accounting and reconciliation

   Security and audit document_access_grants, document_access_events, audit_events, notifications Confidentiality, accountability and operational communication
   Financial database rules

- Use integer minor units for currency amounts where supported by the currency; do not use binary floating-point for money.
- Store currency on every financial amount.
- Use unique external provider transaction and event IDs.
- Make webhook processing idempotent.
- Never delete a confirmed financial event to correct an error; record an adjustment, reversal or correction.
- Keep contractual obligations separate from actual cash movements.
- Keep the provider's actual funds status separate from your platform's own calculated status.
- Reconcile provider transactions and platform ledger entries regularly.
- Restrict changes to payout destinations and require step-up verification and audit logging.
- Do not permit arbitrary edits to ledger entries by administrators.
- Maintain immutable versions of contracts, proposals and approval decisions.
  For a self-hosted first version, I would retain the existing FastAPI modular monolith + PostgreSQL + private object storage architecture. Use separate modules and database transaction boundaries for deals, compliance and payments rather than introducing microservices prematurely.

10. China-specific launch checklist
    These are release gates, not optional future enhancements.
    A. Platform registration and operating model
    Confirm the Chinese operating entity, business scope, platform service agreements, seller/provider verification, transaction rules and applicable platform obligations. The E-Commerce Law addresses platform rules, transaction records, security and dispute-related responsibilities.

MIIT
+1

B. Payment and conditional holding
Obtain written confirmation from the chosen bank/payment institution and Chinese counsel about who receives and holds the funds, who controls release, whether the arrangement is permitted for each deal type, and how refunds, disputes and payouts work. Confirm whether any additional license or approval is required.

pbc.gov.cn
+1

C. Technology transfer and IP
Create a process for verifying rights and screening restricted/prohibited technologies. For applicable cross-border transactions, determine the necessary license, registration and other approvals before permitting the relevant transfer or performance.

Ministry of Commerce China
+2

D. Data protection and security
Complete a PIPL assessment, establish a data-retention schedule, restrict confidential document access, and assess any cross-border transfer of personal information or important data. China’s cross-border data rules provide different mechanisms and exemptions depending on the situation, so do not apply a blanket rule to every transfer.

Supreme People's Procuratorate
+1

E. Contracts and electronic signatures
Validate the contract templates, authority checks, signature/seal process, document integrity and evidence retention for the relevant transaction types.

Cyberspace Administration of China
+1

Data retention
At a minimum, the E-Commerce Law requires covered platform transaction information to be retained for at least three years after transaction completion, subject to other applicable rules. Do not assume that three years is sufficient for every contract, financial record, IP dispute, regulatory filing or legal claim. Apply a retention schedule by record category and preserve relevant evidence when a legal hold applies.

MIIT
+1

Cross-border technology contract registration
The Ministry of Commerce's Measures for the Administration of Registration of Technology Import and Export Contracts cover specified technology import/export contracts and establish registration processes for free-category technologies. The rules provide, among other things, for registration within specified periods after a contract becomes effective, with special treatment for royalty-based payments. Restricted technologies have a different licensing path.

Ministry of Commerce China
+1

Your platform should therefore track a regulatory case against each cross-border contract with fields for:

- Technology classification and review outcome.
- Exporting and importing jurisdictions.
- Required approval, license or registration.
- Responsible party and filing authority.
- Submission date, status, approval document and expiry.
- Contract amendments that may require an updated filing or approval.
- The event that legally permits disclosure, execution, transfer, delivery or payment.
  Do not automatically infer that all cross-border technology contracts require the same registration or that every filing is a condition for contract validity. That depends on the technology classification and applicable rule.

11. Implementation sequence and acceptance tests
    I would build the system in the following order.
1. Phase 1 — Organizations and roles: identity, organization verification, membership, delegated authority and audit events.
1. Phase 2 — Technology and demand: listings, structured demands, review, publication, evidence and search.
1. Phase 3 — Introductions and confidentiality: expressions of interest, consent, controlled disclosure, NDA tracking and communications.
1. Phase 4 — Proposals and contracts: immutable proposal versions, counteroffers, approval routing, contract versions, signature evidence and compliance gates.
1. Phase 5 — Delivery and disputes: milestones, acceptance criteria, evidence, correction cycles, dispute cases and termination.
1. Phase 6 — Payment-provider integration: payment requests, verified provider events, funding allocation, eligible release instructions, payouts, fees, refunds and reconciliation.
1. Phase 7 — Complex deal types: investment, joint ventures, royalties, cross-border contracts and specialized regulatory workflows.
   Minimum acceptance tests before launch
   The system should demonstrate all of the following:

- An unauthorized employee cannot sign, approve a payment or accept a milestone on behalf of an organization.
- A provider cannot disclose a restricted document before the required authorization.
- Changing a proposal after one party accepts it invalidates the old version's status as the agreed final proposal.
- A contract cannot pass a mandatory compliance gate without the required evidence.
- A fake or replayed payment webhook cannot duplicate funding or payout records.
- A buyer's screenshot cannot cause the system to mark a payment as provider-confirmed.
- A milestone cannot release more money than the amount permitted by the obligation and provider-backed arrangement.
- A dispute prevents only the actions that the applicable contract and payment arrangement require it to prevent.
- A failed payout remains failed or pending until reconciled; it is never silently marked paid.
- Refunds, reversals and partial settlements produce auditable financial adjustments.
- Royalties and other continuing obligations survive the closure of the initial delivery workflow.
- An administrator cannot silently rewrite an executed contract, remove financial history or bypass a mandatory regulatory gate.
  Final decision
  For the China launch, the operating model should be:
  Managed discovery → verified authority → consent-based introduction → controlled confidential evaluation → versioned negotiation → authorized contract execution → regulatory clearance → provider-backed funding → milestone delivery and acceptance → authorized release → provider-confirmed payout → reconciliation and continuing-obligation management.
  Support all deal types in the architecture, but apply different contract, compliance, funding and acceptance rules to each type. Keep investment and complex cross-border deals under specialist review.
  Most importantly, treat platform-controlled holding as a legal and payment-provider dependency, not as a feature you can safely implement with a database balance and a release button. That decision determines your payment integration, accounting design, dispute workflow and launch readiness.
