# Technology Exchange & Transfer Platform

## Implementation-Level System Design — Version 1.0

Regional launch

Exchange marketplace

Self-hosted

Small engineering team

Architecture baseline · Domain model · Database · APIs · Workflows · UI · Security · Deployment · Delivery roadmap

The design below translates your selected business direction into a concrete engineering specification. It is intended to be the baseline from which a small development team can create database migrations, backend modules, frontend pages, tests, deployment scripts, and operational procedures.

My primary recommendation is to build a managed technology exchange, rather than a conventional listing website or a fully automated transaction exchange.

The platform should bring together technology suppliers and buyers, help them discover relevant opportunities, verify important claims, control confidential disclosure, facilitate introductions, and manage negotiations through a recorded outcome. The platform's staff will operate the review and exchange workflows, while the participating organizations retain control over their technologies and commercial decisions.

For the initial release, I would deliberately avoid microservices, an independent graph database, a custom payment wallet, and a complex AI platform. Those would add substantial operational work without being necessary to prove the core business.

# 1. Product definition and design assumptions

## 1.1 Agreed product profile

| Decision                | Design                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Initial market          | One regional market, expandable to other regions                                |
| Business model          | Technology exchange marketplace                                                 |
| Initial participants    | Technology suppliers and technology buyers                                      |
| Hosting                 | Self-hosted servers or virtual machines                                         |
| Engineering team        | 1–4 developers                                                                  |
| Transaction scope       | Managed introductions, negotiation and deal tracking                            |
| Industry coverage       | Broad, cross-industry technology                                                |
| Revenue model           | Exchange-related services initially; subscriptions and other revenue can follow |
| Primary operating model | Platform staff support verification, matching and transaction coordination      |

## 1.2 Remaining assumptions

A few decisions are still unresolved. I will make explicit assumptions so that engineering can proceed without pretending these are already settled.

| Unresolved decision   | Baseline assumption                                                                                                 | Consequence                                                                            |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Launch country        | One country, selected before production launch                                                                      | Country-specific legal, tax, privacy and electronic-signature review remains mandatory |
| Launch language       | One primary language, with translation-ready architecture                                                           | User-visible strings and searchable text are language-aware                            |
| Currency              | One settlement/reporting currency initially; records store ISO currency codes                                       | Multi-currency support does not require a redesign                                     |
| Legal status          | Private commercial platform, not a government-recognized statutory exchange unless later authorized                 | Avoid implying official approval or legal certification                                |
| Transaction execution | Platform coordinates deals; parties execute their contracts independently or through an integrated signing provider | No proprietary escrow or custody service                                               |
| Verification          | Human review supported by evidence and external registries where available                                          | Verification claims are scoped and auditable                                           |
| Infrastructure        | Linux servers or VMs under the operator's control                                                                   | Containers and scripted deployment; no Kubernetes initially                            |
| Traffic               | Early-stage traffic with modest initial volume                                                                      | One application deployment, a separate worker process, one primary database            |
| Technology ownership  | Suppliers must declare their authority to list and negotiate                                                        | Publication does not automatically certify ownership                                   |
| AI                    | Optional in later phases                                                                                            | Core business flows must function without AI                                           |

Regional launch is not yet a sufficiently precise legal jurisdiction. The domain model will therefore be jurisdiction-neutral, but production launch must define the country, applicable privacy regime, electronic-signature requirements, export restrictions, and the legal treatment of brokerage and payments.

## 1.3 What the first release will and will not do

### Included in the first production release

Organization accounts and roles

Technology listings and offers

Buyer demands and search

Managed review and matching

Controlled introductions

Deal pipeline and audit trail

### Deferred until justified

- Automated payment settlement or escrow.
- Public auctions and automated bidding.
- Complex commission splitting.
- Dedicated microservices for each domain.
- Custom AI model training and autonomous deal negotiation.
- A separate graph database or event-streaming cluster.
- Multi-region active-active deployment.

# 2. Domain architecture

The most important architectural decision is to model the platform around technology assets, technology offers, buyer demands, matches, introductions and deals.

These are distinct concepts. A technology is not itself an offer, a demand is not a match, and a match is not a transaction.

## 2.1 Core domain entities

### Identity and organizations

User · Organization · Membership · Role · Verification Case

Technology supply

Technology · Version · IP Asset · Offer · Evidence · Documents

Buyer demand

Demand · Requirements · Budget · Constraints · Deadline

### Exchange operations

Match · Shortlist · Introduction Request · Review Task · Communication

### Deal management

Deal · Party · Proposal · Contract Reference · Milestone · Outcome · Dispute

### Cross-cutting platform services

Permissions · Documents · Workflow · Notifications · Audit · Reporting · Billing

## 2.2 The business entities and their responsibilities

| Entity             | Meaning                                                                         | Example                                       |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Organization       | Legal or institutional participant                                              | University, manufacturer, startup             |
| Technology         | Stable identity of a technology asset                                           | High-efficiency industrial coating            |
| Technology version | A specific revision of its description and technical data                       | Revision 3, updated performance data          |
| IP asset           | A patent, patent application, copyright, know-how claim or other relevant right | Patent application in one jurisdiction        |
| Technology offer   | A particular commercial opportunity involving the technology                    | License for a specified territory             |
| Demand             | A buyer's structured problem or need                                            | Reduce industrial inspection time by 30%      |
| Match              | A recorded relationship between a demand and candidate technology               | Demand D matched to Technology T              |
| Introduction       | A controlled request to connect two parties                                     | Buyer requests contact with supplier          |
| Deal               | The managed commercial opportunity                                              | License negotiation between two organizations |
| Evidence           | Support for a claim or review decision                                          | Patent register extract or test report        |
| Review case        | A controlled administrative process                                             | Verify organization or review listing         |
| Milestone          | A deliverable or condition in a deal                                            | Prototype delivery                            |
| Audit event        | A record of a material system action                                            | Listing approved by reviewer                  |

### Critical modeling rule

One technology may have multiple offers:

- A non-exclusive license in Region A.
- An exclusive license in Region B.
- An assignment offer.
- A development partnership.
- A time-limited pilot or evaluation arrangement.

Each offer must define its own transaction type, rights scope, territory, permitted field of use, commercial terms and availability.

The same technology can therefore satisfy multiple demands, but not every offer is compatible with every demand.

## 2.3 Domain boundaries

For the initial modular monolith, define these internal modules:

1. `identity` — users, sessions and authentication.
2. `organizations` — profiles, membership and verification.
3. `taxonomy` — industries, technologies, applications and TRL.
4. `technology` — assets, versions, rights claims and evidence.
5. `offers` — license, assignment and partnership opportunities.
6. `demands` — buyer requirements and constraints.
7. `discovery` — search, filters and candidate retrieval.
8. `matching` — candidate scores, explanations and feedback.
9. `exchange` — shortlists, introduction requests and broker assignments.
10. `deals` — negotiations, proposals, milestones and outcomes.
11. `documents` — private files, access grants and document versions.
12. `workflow` — review queues, tasks, deadlines and transitions.
13. `notifications` — email and in-app notifications.
14. `audit` — immutable business event history.
15. `reporting` — operational and commercial dashboards.

Finance, subscriptions, e-signatures and AI should initially be integration boundaries or later modules, rather than reasons to split the backend into many services.

# 3. Technical architecture

## 3.1 Recommended technology stack

For a small team, I recommend a stack with a clear operational footprint and well-established tooling.

| Layer             | Recommendation                                                                 | Reason                                                           |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Frontend          | React + TypeScript + Vite                                                      | Straightforward SPA development                                  |
| UI                | A consistent component library and shared design tokens                        | Faster screen development                                        |
| Backend           | Python + FastAPI                                                               | Typed APIs, validation and OpenAPI generation                    |
| Domain validation | Pydantic and explicit domain services                                          | Consistent input and business validation                         |
| Primary database  | PostgreSQL                                                                     | Transactions, relational integrity, JSONB and row-level security |
| Search, initial   | PostgreSQL full-text search and trigram indexes                                | Avoid operating another database too early                       |
| Search, later     | OpenSearch or another dedicated search service                                 | Advanced multilingual search and facets                          |
| Cache             | Redis, only when justified                                                     | Rate limits, caching and short-lived coordination                |
| Background jobs   | A persistent database-backed job queue initially                               | Simpler operations for a small team                              |
| File storage      | Private S3-compatible object storage or protected local storage                | Controlled document lifecycle                                    |
| Authentication    | OIDC-compatible identity provider or secure managed application authentication | MFA, session management and integration flexibility              |
| Reverse proxy     | Nginx or Caddy                                                                 | TLS termination and routing                                      |
| Deployment        | Docker Compose on Linux                                                        | Repeatable self-hosted deployment                                |
| Observability     | Structured logs, metrics and error reporting                                   | Operational visibility                                           |
| CI/CD             | Git-based workflow, automated tests and image builds                           | Controlled releases                                              |

The table defines a recommended stack, not a requirement to use every component on day one.

I would start without Redis and without a dedicated search cluster. PostgreSQL can support the first version's search and matching workloads. Add infrastructure when measured performance or product requirements justify it.

For background jobs, use a queue with durable persistence, retry rules, job deduplication and failure visibility. A database-backed queue is reasonable at low volume; move to a broker-backed approach if job volume or delivery guarantees demand it.

For production, I would normally separate the public-facing application from the database host, even if the first environment uses only two or three VMs.

Suggested deployment split:

- VM 1 — Edge and application: reverse proxy, frontend assets and API.
- VM 2 — Data: PostgreSQL and private file/object storage if no dedicated storage server is available.

If there is only one physical server initially, the same logical separation should still be enforced through containers, network rules, separate credentials and persistent volumes.

## 4.2 Database-wide conventions

Use the following conventions consistently:

- UUID primary keys for business entities.
- `TIMESTAMPTZ` for timestamps.
- UTC timestamps in storage; localized presentation in the frontend.
- ISO 4217 currency codes for monetary amounts.
- `NUMERIC` or integer minor units for money, never floating-point arithmetic.
- Foreign keys for stable relational references.
- Database uniqueness constraints for invariants that must hold under concurrency.
- Separate immutable revision records for important published content.
- Separate state-transition records from mutable current-state columns.
- Soft deletion only where justified; preserve legally and operationally necessary history.
- Explicit tenant and resource access checks.
- Database migrations committed to source control.

Do not use free-form status text everywhere. Use constrained values, reference tables or PostgreSQL enums where the state set is stable. For workflows that will evolve frequently, a checked text field plus a centralized domain state machine can be more manageable than repeatedly changing enums.

## 4.3 Core schema — identity and organizations

The following SQL is an executable starting point for the core entities. It assumes PostgreSQL 15 or later and that migrations are applied in dependency order.

```

CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS pgcrypto;

CREATE TABLE users (
    id UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    email TEXT NOT NULL,
    display_name TEXT NOT NULL,
    auth_subject TEXT UNIQUE,
    status TEXT NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pending',
    email_verified_at TIMESTAMPTZ,
    created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    updated_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    CONSTRAINT users_status_chk
        CHECK (status IN (
            'pending', 'active', 'suspended', 'closed'
        ))
);

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX users_email_lower_uq
    ON users (lower(email));

CREATE TABLE organizations (
    id UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    legal_name TEXT NOT NULL,
    display_name TEXT NOT NULL,
    organization_type TEXT NOT NULL,
    jurisdiction_code CHAR(2) NOT NULL,
    registration_number TEXT,
    website_url TEXT,
    description TEXT,
    verification_status TEXT NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pending',
    status TEXT NOT NULL DEFAULT 'active',
    created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    updated_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    CONSTRAINT organization_verification_chk
        CHECK (verification_status IN (
            'pending', 'in_review', 'verified',
            'rejected', 'expired', 'suspended'
        )),
    CONSTRAINT organization_status_chk
        CHECK (status IN ('active', 'suspended', 'closed'))
);

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX organizations_registration_uq
    ON organizations (jurisdiction_code, registration_number)
    WHERE registration_number IS NOT NULL;

CREATE TABLE organization_memberships (
    id UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    organization_id UUID NOT NULL
        REFERENCES organizations(id),
    user_id UUID NOT NULL REFERENCES users(id),
    role_code TEXT NOT NULL,
    status TEXT NOT NULL DEFAULT 'invited',
    invited_by UUID REFERENCES users(id),
    accepted_at TIMESTAMPTZ,
    created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    UNIQUE (organization_id, user_id, role_code),
    CONSTRAINT membership_role_chk
        CHECK (role_code IN (
            'org_owner', 'org_admin',
            'technology_manager', 'demand_manager',
            'deal_manager', 'viewer'
        )),
    CONSTRAINT membership_status_chk
        CHECK (status IN ('invited', 'active', 'revoked'))
);

CREATE INDEX memberships_user_status_idx
    ON organization_memberships (user_id, status);

CREATE INDEX memberships_org_status_idx
    ON organization_memberships (organization_id, status);

```

Implementation notes

1. `auth_subject` identifies the user at the authentication provider. It is not a password field.
2. If authentication is handled internally, store password hashes using a suitable password-hashing library and explicit authentication controls; never store plaintext passwords.
3. The organization registration uniqueness constraint is a duplicate-detection aid, not a substitute for verification.
4. A user may belong to multiple organizations and have different roles in each.
5. Membership revocation must immediately affect subsequent authorization decisions.

The organization roles above are organization-level roles. Platform staff roles should be modeled separately so that an organization administrator cannot accidentally acquire platform-wide privileges.

    demand_version INTEGER NOT NULL,

Matching results should be reproducible. Record the demand version, offer version, matching algorithm version, scoring components and relevant evidence. If a demand changes, create new match records rather than silently rewriting historical results.

# 5. API specifications

The backend should expose a versioned REST API under `/api/v1`. Use generated OpenAPI documentation, typed request and response models, and centralized authentication and authorization dependencies.

## 5.1 API conventions

### Request conventions

- JSON for normal business operations.
- Multipart uploads or short-lived object-storage upload URLs for documents.
- ISO 8601 timestamps with timezone information.
- UUIDs for resource identifiers.
- Cursor or limit/offset pagination, with deterministic sorting.
- Explicit filters rather than opaque query strings.
- `Idempotency-Key` for retriable operations with business effects.
- `If-Match` or an explicit version field for conflict-sensitive edits.

### Response conventions

A normal resource response:

```
{
  "data": {
    "id": "d6efc3d0-9d10-4b0b-a7f8-0dfe39a7f1c1",
    "status": "draft",
    "version": 1
  },
  "meta": {
    "request_id": "7b5f0f5c-85f2-4f48-b3cc-8d5a15d8d0bb"
  }
}
```

An error response:

```
{
  "error": {
    "code": "INVALID_STATE_TRANSITION",
    "message": "The requested action is not allowed.",
    "request_id": "7b5f0f5c-85f2-4f48-b3cc-8d5a15d8d0bb",
    "details": {
      "current_status": "draft",
      "requested_action": "publish"
    }
  }
}
```

Use stable error codes. Do not reveal another organization's confidential resource details when returning authorization errors.

## 6.3 Introduction lifecycle

Recommended states:

`requested → under_review → supplier_pending → approved → introduced → converted_to_deal`

Alternative terminal outcomes include `rejected` and `withdrawn`.

Required business rules:

1. Only a buyer with access to the match can request an introduction.
2. The supplier's disclosure preferences and offer status are checked.
3. The broker may request clarification or reject an unsuitable request.
4. Supplier approval is required unless a documented policy or existing agreement explicitly authorizes otherwise.
5. The platform records the date, parties, disclosure scope and communication channel used.
6. Creating a deal from an introduction must be idempotent.

## 8.3 Authorization model

Every request should evaluate four dimensions:

1. Identity: who is making the request?
2. Role: what actions may that role perform?
3. Relationship: does the user belong to the relevant organization or deal?
4. Resource policy: is the particular version, field, document or action accessible in the current state?

For example, being a `technology_manager` does not automatically permit access to another organization's unpublished technology. Being assigned as a broker to one deal does not automatically permit access to every deal.

## 8.4 Permission implementation

I recommend centralized policy functions, with resource-specific checks:

```
def can_view_technology(actor, technology, context):    if actor.is_authorized_platform_reviewer(context):        return True    if actor.is_active_member(technology.owner_organization_id):        return True    if technology.lifecycle_status != "published":        return False    if technology.visibility != "public":        return context.has_explicit_access_grant(            actor=actor,            resource=technology,        )    return True
```

This is illustrative pseudocode. Production code must also enforce suspension, approved publication snapshots, field-level visibility and any restrictions on the relevant offer.

Authorization should be tested at the service layer and the API boundary. The frontend must never be trusted to enforce the security policy.

## 9.2 Authentication and sessions

Use an OIDC identity provider if feasible, or a well-maintained application authentication library. Require MFA for platform administrators and strongly encourage it for organization owners.

For browser authentication, prefer secure, HttpOnly, SameSite cookies or a correctly implemented OIDC flow. Protect state-changing requests against CSRF when cookie authentication is used. Rotate session identifiers on login and privilege changes, and revoke sessions after account suspension.

## 9.3 Audit and incident response

Audit at least:

- Authentication and privileged access events.
- Organization membership and role changes.
- Verification decisions.
- Publication and suspension.
- Confidential document access and permission changes.
- Introduction approvals and rejections.
- Deal stage transitions.
- Contract evidence and completion decisions.
- Financial adjustments when that module is introduced.
- Policy and configuration changes.

Avoid placing full confidential document contents, passwords, authentication tokens or other secrets in audit logs.

# 10. Search and matching implementation

For the first version, use a hybrid of structured filtering and PostgreSQL full-text search.

## 10.1 Retrieval pipeline

1. Determine which published offers the user is authorized to discover.
2. Apply mandatory filters: offer status, technology visibility, transaction type, territory and other hard constraints.
3. Retrieve candidates through structured indexes and full-text search.
4. Calculate candidate scores from technical fit, maturity, commercial fit, rights compatibility, evidence quality and deployment constraints.
5. Persist the match record and ranking version.
6. Present the best candidates with explanations.
7. Capture shortlist and rejection feedback.
8. Allow a broker to review high-value or uncertain candidates.

The system should never rely on a single embedding similarity score to determine legal or commercial eligibility.

## 10.2 Initial ranking model

An initial configurable score could be:

\\[ S=0.35T+0.20M+0.15C+0.15R+0.10E+0.05G \\]

Where the variables represent normalized technical fit, maturity, commercial fit, rights compatibility, evidence quality and geographic compatibility.

These weights are hypotheses for testing, not validated industry benchmarks.

Mandatory constraints should be applied before ranking. A rights incompatibility should result in ineligibility or manual review, not simply a lower score.

## 10.3 Matching quality loop

For every recommendation, preserve:

- Demand version.
- Offer and technology version.
- Candidate retrieval method.
- Ranking algorithm version.
- Individual score components.
- Relevant evidence.
- Broker decision.
- Buyer feedback.
- Eventual deal outcome, if known.

This enables evaluation against expert decisions and completed transactions. Without it, the team cannot determine whether matching improvements actually improve deal conversion.

# 11. Self-hosted infrastructure and deployment

## 11.1 Recommended starting topology

For a small team, I recommend Linux VMs with Docker Compose rather than Kubernetes.

### VM 1 — Edge and application

Reverse proxy · TLS · React frontend · FastAPI · authentication integration

### VM 2 — Data services

PostgreSQL · private object storage · persistent job storage

### VM 3 — Optional worker and operations

Background worker · document scanner · scheduled tasks · metrics and log forwarding

Introduce when load or security isolation justifies it.

Separate backup target

Encrypted database backups, object-storage backups, recovery credentials and restore tests.

## 11.2 Network layout

- Public internet: only the reverse proxy on required HTTP/HTTPS ports.
- Application network: API, worker and internal services.
- Data network: PostgreSQL and private object storage, reachable only from authorized hosts.
- Administration network: SSH and management access restricted to approved addresses or a VPN.
- Backup network or target: separate credentials and restricted access.

Do not expose PostgreSQL, Redis, object-storage administration or internal worker ports directly to the public internet.

## 11.3 Initial resource planning

The following are planning estimates, not measured capacity guarantees.

| Component          | Starting allocation                                       |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Application VM     | 2–4 vCPU, 4–8 GB RAM                                      |
| Data VM            | 4–8 vCPU, 8–16 GB RAM                                     |
| Optional worker VM | 2–4 vCPU, 4–8 GB RAM                                      |
| Database storage   | SSD/NVMe sized for data, indexes, WAL and growth          |
| Document storage   | Separate capacity budget with lifecycle and backup policy |
| Backup storage     | Separate failure domain where practical                   |

Actual sizing depends on document volume, search load, number of organizations, indexing, concurrency, and retention requirements. Measure CPU, memory, I/O latency, database connections, queue backlog and response times before increasing capacity.

## 11.5 Release and recovery process

Every release should follow a controlled sequence:

1. Build and test the release artifact.
2. Run automated unit, integration and authorization tests.
3. Back up the database and verify backup health.
4. Apply backward-compatible database migrations.
5. Deploy the application and worker versions.
6. Run health checks and smoke tests.
7. Verify queue processing, file access and search.
8. Monitor errors and performance.
9. Roll back application versions if needed; use a tested database recovery plan if a migration is not safely reversible.

Do not rely on automatic database rollback for every schema change. Prefer expand-and-contract migrations for changes that span multiple releases.

# 12. Testing and acceptance criteria

Testing must cover business rules, not merely whether the endpoints return HTTP 200.

## 12.1 Critical end-to-end scenarios

| Scenario                                | Expected result                                                 |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Supplier submits a technology           | A review case is created and the draft is not publicly visible  |
| Reviewer requests corrections           | Supplier sees required changes; no public publication occurs    |
| Reviewer approves a version             | Only approved fields are published and indexed                  |
| Buyer submits a demand                  | Demand version and review state are recorded                    |
| Matching runs twice                     | No duplicate side effects; ranking results are versioned        |
| Buyer requests an introduction          | The request is assigned to the appropriate review workflow      |
| Supplier rejects an introduction        | No deal is automatically created                                |
| Introduction is approved                | Only the authorized disclosure scope is shared                  |
| Unauthorized user guesses a document ID | Access is denied                                                |
| Technology is suspended                 | New discovery and introductions are blocked                     |
| Two reviewers decide simultaneously     | Only one valid state transition succeeds                        |
| Notification delivery fails             | The business operation remains committed and delivery can retry |
| Deal reaches completion                 | Required deal-specific completion conditions are satisfied      |
| Backup is restored                      | Database and required document references remain consistent     |

## 12.2 Security release blockers

At minimum, automate tests proving that:

- Organization A cannot read Organization B's private technology, demand or documents.
- Revoked members lose access.
- Public search never exposes unpublished or suspended listings.
- Confidential document text does not leak through search snippets or AI-generated explanations.
- Users cannot change protected statuses through general-purpose update endpoints.
- Retried requests cannot duplicate introductions or deals.
- Reviewer permissions cannot be escalated by modifying client payloads.
- Administrative actions require appropriate authorization and audit records.

# 14. Operational workflows and service-level targets

A managed exchange needs defined service standards, even before the platform has a large operations team.

These are proposed initial targets, not established industry benchmarks.

| Operational process       | Initial target                                         | Escalation                    |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Organization verification | First review within 3 business days                    | Escalate overdue cases        |
| Technology review         | First review within 5 business days                    | Notify reviewer manager       |
| Correction request        | Acknowledge within 2 business days                     | Escalate unanswered cases     |
| Introduction request      | Initial response within 2 business days                | Reassign stalled cases        |
| Match review              | Review high-priority candidates within 2 business days | Escalate urgent buyer demand  |
| Deal milestone            | Remind before due date                                 | Escalate overdue milestones   |
| Security incident         | Immediate triage according to severity                 | Follow incident response plan |

Use configurable targets and business calendars. Measure time to first response separately from total resolution time, and exclude periods waiting for information from users when calculating internal processing performance.

The operations dashboard should show the age of each case, current owner, next action and deadline. A count of pending items without aging or ownership information is not enough.

# 16. Business metrics to instrument from day one

The first release should record the events needed to measure whether the exchange creates real value.

### Exchange funnel

Verified suppliers and active buyers

Published technologies and qualified demands

Qualified matches and shortlists

Approved introductions and evaluations

Negotiated and completed transfers

Track conversion and time between stages, as well as reasons for failure. These records will show whether the bottleneck is insufficient supply, poor matching, weak evidence, slow reviews, incompatible terms or difficult negotiations.

For commercial reporting, separate completed transactions from estimated deal values and distinguish platform-earned revenue from money paid directly between counterparties.

I would begin with the following concrete engineering tasks rather than immediately building the full marketplace UI.

| Order | Task                                      | Definition of done                                                        |
| ----- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Establish repository and coding standards | Reproducible frontend/backend development environment                     |
| 2     | Create deployment baseline                | Application starts in a repeatable self-hosted environment                |
| 3     | Add database migrations                   | Core identity and organization schema can be built from an empty database |
| 4     | Implement authentication                  | Protected API routes and secure sessions work                             |
| 5     | Implement organization membership         | Role checks operate at the backend                                        |
| 6     | Implement audit events                    | Sensitive actions generate structured records                             |
| 7     | Build authorization test suite            | Cross-organization access tests pass                                      |
| 8     | Create initial UI shell                   | Navigation and permission-aware pages are in place                        |

The first vertical slice should be small but complete:

Register user → create organization → create technology → submit for review → request correction → resubmit → approve → publish.

This validates the most important architectural foundations: identity, tenant isolation, versioning, state transitions, review operations, audit history and public disclosure.

Once this slice works reliably, demand management and matching can be added on top of proven foundations.

## Final engineering decision

I would adopt the following as the baseline architecture:

- Frontend: React and TypeScript.
- Backend: FastAPI modular monolith.
- Database: PostgreSQL with explicit relational constraints and migrations.
- Search: PostgreSQL full-text search initially.
- Documents: private object storage with quarantine and access grants.
- Workflow: explicit state machines, persisted tasks and an audit trail.
- Deployment: Linux VMs and Docker Compose.
- Business model: managed exchange with technology offers, buyer demands, introductions and deal management.
- Security model: organization-level roles combined with resource-level permissions.
- Delivery strategy: complete one vertical business workflow at a time.

The biggest risk is not choosing the wrong framework. It is failing to distinguish claims from verified facts, listings from offers, matches from introductions, and signed contracts from completed transfers. Those distinctions must be enforced in the database, API, workflow engine, administrator console and reporting system from the beginning.

The one business decision I would resolve before the production build is the exact launch country or region. It will determine which organization-verification sources, IP evidence requirements, privacy controls, contract processes and transaction restrictions must be implemented. Everything else above provides a workable technical baseline while that decision is finalized.
