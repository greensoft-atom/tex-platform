# Technology Exchange & Transfer Platform — Complete System Design Blueprint

My recommendation is to design this as a technology commercialization and exchange platform, combining the strongest business concepts of Shanghai Technology Exchange (STEX) and NETC/InnoMatch with a modern, internationally scalable software architecture.

I would approach this as a principal system architect responsible for the entire product: business strategy, domain modeling, user experience, transaction integrity, security, operations, revenue, and long-term scalability.

The objective is not merely to build a website where universities upload patents and companies search for them. It is to build a platform that helps technologies move from discovery and research into licensing, acquisition, investment, pilot projects, and commercial deployment.

This is a substantial product, so the design below establishes the full target architecture and workflows, while distinguishing what should be implemented in the first release from capabilities that should be introduced later.

### The seven design principles

1. Demand-driven: corporate requirements should be as important as technology listings.
2. Trust before transactions: verify organizations, ownership claims, intellectual property, and technical evidence.
3. Confidentiality by default: unpublished inventions and trade secrets must not become public merely because a listing exists.
4. Human accountability: AI can recommend, summarize, and prioritize, but cannot independently certify ownership or approve regulated transactions.
5. Deal-centric workflows: a technology inquiry should become a traceable business process, not disappear into email.
6. Multiple revenue streams: transaction fees, enterprise subscriptions, scouting, due diligence, evaluation, and managed services.
7. Incremental implementation: start with a reliable marketplace and transfer workflow; introduce regulated exchange and financial capabilities when the legal and operational foundations exist.

### Key strategic decision: one platform, three business layers

| Layer                          | Responsibility                                                                        | Business outcome                               |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Discovery marketplace          | Publish, search, match technologies and demands                                       | Qualified opportunities                        |
| Transfer management            | Confidentiality, diligence, negotiation, contracts, deal coordination                 | Completed technology deals                     |
| Exchange and commercialization | Formal listings, auctions, transaction certification, settlement integrations, pilots | Trusted transactions and deployed technologies |

These layers should share one identity system, technology registry, organization model, permissions framework, and deal record. They should not be developed as three disconnected products.

Important distinction: an ordinary commercial marketplace can record agreements and coordinate transactions. Official exchange status, legally effective rights registration, regulated escrow, and financial settlement may require separate authorization, licensed partners, or jurisdiction-specific compliance. The architecture must make those boundaries explicit.

## 3.1 Recommended architecture: modular monolith first

For the first production release, I would choose a modular monolith with asynchronous background workers, rather than immediately adopting dozens of microservices.

Why?

A new marketplace has uncertain demand, evolving business rules, and a need for fast iteration. A modular monolith gives you:

- Transactional consistency for deals, permissions, and workflow state.
- Simpler deployment and debugging.
- Fewer distributed transactions.
- Lower initial infrastructure and operational costs.
- A clear migration path to independent services when actual scale justifies it.

Each module should own its domain model and data access. Modules must not directly manipulate another module's tables as an informal shortcut.

The architecture can later separate search, document processing, AI matching, notifications, analytics, and payment integrations.

Important modeling rule: technology, offer, demand, deal, contract, and payment are different entities. Do not put all their fields into one enormous `technology` table.

A technology can have multiple offers, multiple interested buyers, several demands, and multiple historical deals. An offer can change without rewriting the underlying technology identity. A deal can cover multiple technologies and rights.

## 4.2 Technology is not the same thing as intellectual property

A single technology may include:

- Several patents and pending applications.
- Unpatented trade secrets and know-how.
- Software or model components.
- Test results and prototype evidence.
- Manufacturing processes.
- Third-party components and dependencies.

Each IP asset should have its own owner or claimed rights holder, jurisdiction, registration identifiers, legal status, evidence, and review history.

The platform must distinguish between owner asserted, document checked, independently verified, and legally confirmed. These are different claims.

### TRL must be evidence-backed

Use a recognized TRL scale, typically 1–9, with a documented definition for each level. Store the claimed level separately from the independently assessed level. A laboratory prototype should not automatically be treated as industrially validated.

I would also introduce a commercial-readiness assessment, but keep it distinct from TRL. Manufacturing readiness, regulatory readiness, IP position, customer validation, and unit economics are different dimensions.

# 5. Roles, permissions and account structure

Do not implement permissions as a single `role` column on the user.

One person may be a technology owner at a university, a buyer at a startup, and a technical expert in another context. Access should depend on the organization, resource, action, and deal.

## 5.1 External roles

| Role                                  | Main responsibilities                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Individual inventor                   | Submit technology and evidence; respond to inquiries subject to ownership authorization |
| University technology-transfer office | Manage institutional IP, approve disclosures and negotiate licenses                     |
| Technology supplier                   | Publish authorized offers and manage buyer relationships                                |
| Corporate buyer                       | Create demands, discover technologies, manage evaluation and procurement                |
| R&D manager                           | Define requirements and assess technical fit                                            |
| Technology broker/manager             | Manage assigned matching and transfer cases                                             |
| Technical expert                      | Review assigned evidence and submit assessments                                         |
| IP/legal professional                 | Conduct scoped legal and rights diligence                                               |
| Investor                              | Discover opportunities and review authorized investment information                     |
| Service provider                      | Offer evaluation, prototyping, regulatory or commercialization services                 |
| Enterprise administrator              | Manage their organization's users, policies, subscriptions and permissions              |

## 5.2 Internal roles

| Role                              | Main responsibilities                                                |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Platform administrator            | Global configuration, access control and operational oversight       |
| Organization verification officer | Verify organizational identities and authority                       |
| Technology review officer         | Review completeness, quality and publication suitability             |
| IP verification specialist        | Review IP documentation within authorized scope                      |
| Deal operations manager           | Monitor transaction workflow and unblock operational tasks           |
| Finance/reconciliation officer    | Reconcile invoices, payments and refunds                             |
| Dispute officer                   | Manage complaints and conflict-of-interest controls                  |
| Platform commercial manager       | Pricing, enterprise accounts, service packages and revenue reporting |
| Compliance/privacy officer        | Data requests, retention, access reviews and regulatory processes    |
| Security auditor                  | Review security events and privileged actions                        |

### Permission model

I recommend a hybrid of role-based access control (RBAC) and attribute/resource-based access control (ABAC).

For example, a broker may read a deal only if:

- They have the appropriate broker role.
- The deal is assigned to them or their authorized team.
- Their organization is permitted to act in that jurisdiction.
- The deal has not been restricted by a conflict or legal hold.
- The requested document is within their assigned scope.

Sensitive actions should require explicit permission checks on the backend. Hiding a button in React is not an authorization control.

### Detailed execution

1. The supplier creates an account and accepts the applicable platform terms.
2. The supplier creates an organization profile or requests membership in an existing organization.
3. The platform verifies identity, organizational existence, and the user's authority to act for the organization.
4. The supplier creates a technology draft and chooses its technology type, applications, maturity, and intended transaction model.
5. The system requests relevant IP records, technical evidence, and rights declarations.
6. Automated checks identify missing fields, inconsistent claims, unsupported TRL assertions, potentially sensitive documents, and duplicate technologies.
7. The supplier submits the record to a review queue.
8. A reviewer evaluates completeness, evidence quality, publication permissions, and any applicable restrictions.
9. The reviewer approves, rejects, or requests corrections.
10. The platform generates a versioned publication snapshot containing only approved fields.
11. Search indexes the approved snapshot and the matching engine evaluates relevant demands.
12. The supplier can update the record, but material changes require the appropriate review before becoming public.

### Important rule

Do not force every demand into a technology purchase.

Some demands are best fulfilled by:

- A new research collaboration.

## 7.3 Workflow C: matching and recommendation

The system should use a staged matching pipeline.

Stage 1 — Eligibility filters

Rights availability, geography, required TRL, prohibited sectors, budget range, and mandatory technical constraints.

Stage 2 — Candidate retrieval

Structured database filters, keyword search, multilingual search, semantic retrieval, and taxonomy relationships.

Stage 3 — Ranking and evidence

Technical fit, maturity, deployment constraints, rights compatibility, evidence quality, and commercial terms.

Stage 4 — Human review

A broker or qualified specialist reviews the best candidates, rejects false positives, and identifies missing information.

Stage 5 — Controlled introduction

The system requests approval before disclosing confidential details or introducing the parties.

### Matching score design

An initial ranking model might be:

\\[ S = 0.35T + 0.20M + 0.15C + 0.15R + 0.10E + 0.05G \\]

Where each component is normalized from 0 to 100:

- \\(T\\): technical fit.
- \\(M\\): maturity and readiness.
- \\(C\\): commercial fit.
- \\(R\\): rights and transaction compatibility.
- \\(E\\): evidence quality.
- \\(G\\): geographic and deployment compatibility.

These weights are illustrative starting assumptions, not validated performance data. They should be configurable, sector-specific where appropriate, and tested against actual expert judgments and completed deals.

Mandatory constraints should not be compensated for by a high score elsewhere. For example, a technology that lacks a legally necessary right should not rank as eligible simply because its technical score is excellent.

The interface should show why a technology was recommended, what evidence supports the recommendation, and which gaps remain. Never display a numerical match score as if it were a probability of commercial success.

# 9. The administrator's business and operating workflows

This is where many marketplace designs are incomplete. A platform with a polished public website can still fail because no one owns verification queues, transaction exceptions, disputes, or supplier quality.

## 9.1 Operations control center

The administrator homepage should be an actionable workbench.

Organization verification

# Pending

Applications, authority checks, escalations

Technology review

# Pending

New listings, corrections, evidence reviews

Deal operations

# At risk

Overdue diligence, stalled negotiations, conditions

Finance exceptions

# Reconciliation

Failed payments, refunds, invoices, disputes

These labels are examples of dashboard widgets, not live platform statistics.

## 9.2 Organization verification workflow

1. Receive an application and assign a case ID.
2. Check legal identity against reliable sources in the applicable jurisdiction.
3. Verify the applicant's authority to represent the organization.
4. Request additional documentation where necessary.
5. Conduct sanctions, fraud or other checks where legally applicable.
6. Approve, reject or escalate.
7. Record the evidence source, review date, scope and expiry/recheck requirements.
8. Notify the applicant and record any appeal.

Organization verification does not prove ownership of every technology the organization uploads. Technology rights must be assessed separately.

## 9.3 Technology review workflow

Every review should use a checklist appropriate to the technology type.

| Check               | Example evidence                                              |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness        | Required technical fields populated                           |
| Ownership/authority | Assignment, inventor declaration, institutional authorization |
| IP claims           | Patent register data and relevant documents                   |
| Technical claims    | Publications, test reports, specifications                    |
| Maturity            | Prototype and validation evidence                             |
| Publication safety  | Confidentiality and third-party information review            |
| Offer authority     | Permission to license, assign or negotiate                    |
| Duplication         | Existing technology record or competing submissions           |
| Restricted content  | Export controls, sanctions or other relevant restrictions     |

Reviewers should be able to approve the public profile while restricting confidential attachments. They should not have to approve or reject the entire technology as one indivisible object.

## 9.4 Matching operations workflow

AI-generated matches need quality control.

- Review low-confidence or high-value candidates.
- Detect false positives caused by misleading terminology.
- Identify demands with no viable candidates.
- Assign experts for manual scouting when necessary.
- Record why a candidate was rejected.
- Collect buyer feedback and eventual deal outcomes.
- Compare predicted relevance with human judgments.
- Tune matching weights against actual results.

Do not allow a high-value paying client to silently influence technical match rankings. Commercial prioritization and technical relevance should be separate, disclosed dimensions.

## 9.5 Transaction operations workflow

The transaction operations team should monitor:

- Contracts awaiting approval.
- Signatures and conditions outstanding.
- Invoice and payment status.
- Payment reconciliation discrepancies.
- Rights-transfer evidence and delivery obligations.
- Failed external integrations.
- Refunds and cancellations.
- Disputes, holds and legal restrictions.

An administrator should not be able to bypass every control using a single “Force Complete” button. Exceptional actions should require a specific permission, a reason, supporting evidence and an audit record; high-risk transitions may require dual approval.

## 9.6 Admin state-transition matrix

| Action                          | Normal owner                       | Required controls                          |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Approve organization            | Verification officer               | Evidence and review record                 |
| Approve technology publication  | Technology reviewer                | Checklist and publication scope            |
| Change IP verification status   | Authorized specialist              | Evidence, scope and review date            |
| Assign broker                   | Operations manager                 | Conflict-of-interest check                 |
| Change deal stage               | Deal owner / authorized operator   | Preconditions and audit event              |
| Mark payment reconciled         | Finance officer/system integration | Bank/provider evidence                     |
| Approve refund                  | Finance role                       | Original payment reference and policy      |
| Resolve dispute                 | Authorized dispute officer         | Evidence, rationale and required approvals |
| Change global permission policy | Platform security administrator    | Change review and audit trail              |

# 10. Detailed user stories and acceptance criteria

The following are examples of production-oriented requirements. Each should ultimately become a ticket with tests, design references, API contracts, and measurable acceptance criteria.

## Epic A — Identity and organizations

US-001: Register an account

As a prospective participant, I want to register and verify my email so that I can access the platform.

Acceptance criteria:

- A user cannot access protected functions before the required verification is complete.
- Repeated registration requests do not create duplicate accounts.
- Rate limits and abuse detection protect authentication endpoints.
- The user can recover access through a controlled process.
- Every authentication event is logged appropriately without storing plaintext passwords or secrets.

US-002: Create an organization

As a company representative, I want to create an organization profile so that I can manage technologies or demands on behalf of my company.

Acceptance criteria:

- Organization type, jurisdiction, legal name and relevant identifiers are captured.
- Duplicate organizations are flagged for review.
- The creator receives only the initial permissions granted by the account policy.
- Verification status is displayed accurately.
- Organization membership can be revoked without deleting the historical activity.

US-003: Invite a colleague

As an organization administrator, I want to invite colleagues with specific roles.

Acceptance criteria:

- Invitations expire and cannot be reused after acceptance.
- Invitees must authenticate and accept the invitation.
- Organization administrators cannot grant permissions beyond their own delegated authority.
- Membership changes invalidate affected sessions or permissions where appropriate.

## Epic B — Technology registration

US-010: Create a technology

As a technology owner, I want to create a draft with technical, commercial and IP information.

Acceptance criteria:

- The user can save an incomplete draft.
- Mandatory fields vary by technology type.
- Uploaded files are stored privately until access is explicitly authorized.
- Every submitted version has a stable identifier.
- The platform preserves material change history.

US-011: Submit for publication

As an authorized supplier, I want to submit a technology for review.

Acceptance criteria:

- Submission captures the exact version reviewed.
- A review case is created once, even if the client retries the request.
- The supplier can view review status and requested corrections.
- Reviewers can approve only fields within their permission scope.
- Only approved fields become publicly searchable.

## Epic C — Demand and matching

US-020: Create a demand

As an R&D manager, I want to define a technical challenge with measurable requirements.

Acceptance criteria:

- Hard constraints are distinguished from preferences.
- Budget, target dates and geography can be private.
- The demand can be saved as a draft and reviewed before publication.
- The system rejects invalid or contradictory hard constraints or requests clarification.
- Demand edits create a new version and trigger rematching when relevant.

US-021: Review recommendations

As a buyer, I want to see technologies that match my requirements and understand why they were recommended.

Acceptance criteria:

- Each recommendation includes an explanation and relevant evidence.
- Disqualifying constraints are visible.
- Results respect access permissions and publication restrictions.
- The buyer can shortlist, reject or request an introduction.
- Rejection reasons can be captured as structured feedback.

## Epic D — Confidentiality and deal management

US-030: Request confidential information

As a buyer, I want to request access to restricted technical information.

Acceptance criteria:

- The supplier controls disclosure approval unless an explicit prior authorization applies.
- The platform records the applicable confidentiality agreement and its status.
- Access is checked on every document request.
- Access expires or is revoked according to policy.
- Confidential files are excluded from public search indexing.

US-031: Open a deal

As a buyer or authorized broker, I want to create a deal from a qualified opportunity.

Acceptance criteria:

- The deal references the relevant technology and/or demand.
- Parties and authorized representatives are identified.
- The initial stage and assigned owner are recorded.
- All parties receive only the access they are entitled to.
- A deal cannot progress through prohibited transitions.

US-032: Complete a transfer

As the parties to a deal, we want to record the executed agreement and completion conditions.

Acceptance criteria:

- The executed contract version is immutable within the audit record.
- Required approvals and signatures are verified.
- Completion conditions are checked for the particular deal type.
- Payment status comes from authorized evidence or an integration.
- Disputed or suspended deals cannot be completed through the normal path.

## Epic E — Administration

US-040: Process a review queue

As a review officer, I want to claim, assess and resolve assigned cases.

Acceptance criteria:

- Cases have an owner, priority, due date and history.
- Review decisions require a reason and applicable checklist.
- Conflicts of interest can be declared and trigger reassignment.
- Overdue cases can be escalated.
- Review metrics can be calculated from recorded timestamps.

US-041: Reconcile transactions

As a finance officer, I want to reconcile invoices, payment provider records and platform transaction records.

Acceptance criteria:

- Each payment maps to an internal transaction reference.
- Duplicate provider events do not create duplicate accounting entries.
- Amount, currency and status discrepancies are flagged.
- Refunds reference their original transactions.
- Reconciliation adjustments require authorization and a recorded reason.

## Epic F — Commercial services

US-050: Purchase a technology scouting service

As an enterprise client, I want to commission a structured search for technologies that meet a business requirement.

Acceptance criteria:

- The service defines scope, deliverables, deadlines, price and acceptance criteria.
- The platform assigns an authorized analyst or broker.
- Candidate technologies are traceable to evidence and search criteria.
- Deliverables are accessible only to authorized client users.
- The service can lead to a deal without requiring the client to republish confidential information.

These epics provide an initial backlog. They are not a substitute for a full set of acceptance tests covering each state transition, permissions boundary, integration failure, and exception path.

# 11. Database and data architecture

I would begin with PostgreSQL and a clearly normalized transactional schema, then add dedicated search and analytics systems as needed.

## 11.1 Core database conventions

Every major business table should generally have:

- A stable primary key, preferably UUID.
- `created_at` and `updated_at`.
- An organization or tenant ownership field where applicable.
- An explicit status.
- A version number or immutable revision record where history matters.
- Appropriate foreign keys and uniqueness constraints.
- Indexed fields for common access patterns.
- Audit events for sensitive business transitions.

Do not put every field into JSON. Use relational columns for core business relationships, state, rights, money and permissions. Use JSONB for flexible technical attributes, extraction metadata, and less frequently queried extensions.

### Illustrative schema

The following is a design excerpt, not a complete deployable schema.

```

CREATE TABLE organizations (

    legal_name TEXT NOT NULL,
    organization_type TEXT NOT NULL,

### What is intentionally missing?

A production schema also needs separate tables for:

- Users and authentication identities.
- Technology offers and IP assets.
- Versioned demands and matching results.
- Verification cases, evidence and decisions.
- Deals, parties, negotiations and communications.
- NDAs, data rooms and document permissions.
- Contract versions, signatures and completion conditions.
- Invoices, payments, refunds and reconciliation.
- Service engagements and milestones.
- Subscription entitlements and billing.
- Notifications, workflow tasks and idempotency keys.
- Disputes, legal holds and retention requirements.

Use database migrations from the beginning. Never make production schema changes manually without a reproducible migration and rollback/recovery plan.

## 11.2 Multi-tenant isolation

If many organizations use the same platform, tenant isolation is a first-class security requirement.

For shared infrastructure:

1. Every tenant-owned resource must have an explicit ownership or access relationship.
2. Backend queries must enforce that relationship.
3. PostgreSQL Row-Level Security (RLS) can provide an additional protection layer.
4. Privileged administrative access must be separate from normal user access.
5. Background jobs must carry and validate their tenant/resource scope.
6. Search results, caches, exports and document URLs must obey the same access policy.

RLS is defense in depth, not a replacement for application authorization. Use a restricted database role for the application and carefully control any privileged or table-owner access that could bypass RLS.

## 11.3 Search architecture

Use separate representations for transactional data and search.

- PostgreSQL: authoritative technology, demand, rights, deal and permission records.
- Search index: approved searchable fields, filters, facets, multilingual text and ranking signals.
- Vector index: embeddings and semantic similarity.
- Object storage: original documents and extracted files.
- Analytics store: aggregated metrics and historical outcomes.

The search index is a derived representation. It must never be the authority for whether a user can access a confidential document.

When a technology is suspended or its visibility changes, the system must propagate that change to every relevant index and cache. Use a transactional outbox or an equivalent reliable event mechanism so updates are not lost if indexing fails.

## 11.4 Document architecture

Technical documents can contain the most valuable assets on the platform. Design their lifecycle explicitly.

```
Upload request
     |
     v
Private quarantine storage
     |
     v
File validation and malware scan
     |
     v
Text extraction / metadata extraction
     |
     v
Access classification and review
     |
     v
Approved private storage
     |
     +----> Authorized search fields
     |
     +----> Restricted deal room
     |
     +----> Versioned evidence record
```

The extraction pipeline should never publish document text automatically. It should also account for embedded metadata, hidden spreadsheet content, document revisions, and other unintended disclosures.

## 12.3 API implementation rules

- Validate inputs at the API boundary and again at critical domain transitions.
- Use an idempotency key for requests that may be retried, especially deal creation, payment initiation, refunds and external callbacks.
- Apply authorization to every resource access.
- Return structured error codes and safe error messages.
- Use pagination, filtering and deterministic sorting.
- Apply rate limits to authentication, search and expensive AI operations.
- Use optimistic concurrency control when multiple users can edit the same record.
- Version APIs and document their contracts.
- Do not trust frontend-supplied prices, fees, organization roles or completion status.
- Publish domain events only after the underlying business change has committed.

# 13. Workflow engine and state-transition implementation

I would not implement business workflows as scattered conditions across controllers.

Each workflow should be a state machine with explicit transition rules.

For example, a deal transition may be modeled as:

```
def transition_deal(deal, command, actor, policy):    current_state = deal.status    # Verify actor, resource access and assigned responsibility.    policy.require_permission(        actor=actor,        resource=deal,        action=command.action,    )    # Resolve the transition from a controlled transition table.    transition = transition_table.get(        (current_state, command.action)    )    if transition is None:        raise InvalidTransition(            f"Action is not allowed from {current_state}"        )    # Enforce domain-specific prerequisites.    transition.validate(deal, command)    # Update state and append the transition record    # within one database transaction.    return transition.execute(deal, command, actor)
```

This is illustrative pseudocode, not production-ready code.

### Required workflow capabilities

- Allowed transitions and their prerequisites.
- Role and attribute-based authorization.
- Task assignment and reassignment.
- Deadlines, reminders and escalation.
- Approval chains and dual control.
- Idempotent commands.
- Immutable transition history.
- Retryable external actions.
- Timeout and exception handling.
- Versioned workflow definitions where old cases must continue under their original rules.

For the initial release, state machines implemented in the application and persisted in PostgreSQL are usually sufficient. Consider a dedicated workflow engine such as Temporal when workflows become long-running, distributed, timer-heavy, and dependent on reliable retries across many systems.

# 14. Revenue, pricing and financial architecture

I would build the commercial model into the domain design from the beginning, even if the first release uses only two or three paid services.

## 14.1 Revenue streams

A. Enterprise subscriptions

Recurring revenue from private technology scouting, advanced search, multiple users, team workflows, analytics, saved demands and API access.

B. Transaction services

Fixed transaction charges, negotiated service fees, or success-based fees where legally permissible and contractually agreed.

C. Managed technology scouting

Paid programs to find, shortlist, evaluate and introduce technologies for a corporate client.

D. Professional services

Due diligence, evaluation, valuation, IP review, pilot coordination, technical consulting and commercialization support.

E. Institutional and network services

University or regional innovation programs, sponsored scouting, technology-transfer office tools, and contracted platform operations.

## 14.2 Do not treat transaction value as revenue

The finance model must distinguish:

- Gross contract value.
- Platform fee earned.
- Professional-service revenue.
- Third-party service costs.
- Amounts collected on behalf of another party.
- Refunds and reversals.
- Taxes.
- Recognized revenue and deferred revenue.
- Outstanding receivables and payables.

If a license contract is worth €1 million and the platform earns a €20,000 service fee, the platform's revenue is not €1 million.

Likewise, money held or transmitted on behalf of parties must not automatically be recorded as platform revenue. Depending on the legal structure, payment flows may require licensed payment providers and specific accounting treatment.

## 14.3 Commercial design for the first year

I would test these offerings rather than impose a single commission model:

| Offering                       | Initial monetization approach         |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Basic supplier profile         | Free                                  |
| Basic technology publication   | Free, subject to review rules         |
| Basic demand publication       | Free or limited free tier             |
| Enterprise discovery workspace | Annual or monthly subscription        |
| Managed scouting project       | Fixed project fee                     |
| Technical evaluation           | Fixed fee or scoped statement of work |
| Due diligence                  | Fixed fee or staged engagement        |
| Transaction coordination       | Contractually agreed fee              |
| API and bulk data access       | Enterprise license                    |

The key is to avoid charging for every action before there is sufficient supply and demand. Monetization should be tied to measurable value, particularly for enterprise buyers.

## 14.4 Finance implementation

Use a separate financial domain with:

- Immutable invoice and payment references.
- A defined currency and decimal precision.
- A ledger or accounting integration suitable for the business's needs.
- Explicit transaction fees and fee-calculation versions.
- Reconciliation against authoritative provider or bank records.
- Refund, reversal and dispute workflows.
- Separation of revenue, receivables, payables and third-party funds.
- Audit trails for all financial adjustments.

Do not use a floating-point number for money. Use fixed-precision decimal values or integer minor units, with explicit currency handling.

Do not build an internal wallet or escrow service without first resolving the applicable legal and licensing requirements.

# 15. AI and technology intelligence architecture

AI should enhance the platform's workflow, not become an isolated chatbot that has no authority over business records.

## 15.1 AI capabilities

| Capability                | Input                                  | Output                                    | Human control                     |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |

| Technology classification | Technology description                 | Taxonomy suggestions                      | Supplier or reviewer confirms     |
| Demand structuring        | Natural-language business problem      | Structured demand fields                  | Buyer confirms constraints        |
| Semantic matching         | Approved demand and technology content | Candidate rankings and explanations       | Broker reviews important matches  |
| Due diligence assistance  | Authorized evidence                    | Questions, inconsistencies and risk flags | Qualified specialist decides      |
| Translation               | Approved content                       | Multilingual draft                        | Review sensitive material         |
| Deal assistance           | Authorized deal documents              | Summaries and action lists                | Authorized party approves actions |
| Market intelligence       | Licensed and authorized datasets       | Trends and comparable evidence            | Analyst validates methodology     |

## 15.2 Recommended AI pipeline

```
Authorized source documents
          |
          v
Document classification
          |
          v
Text extraction and parsing
          |
          v
Structured metadata extraction
          |
          v
Validation and evidence linking
          |
          v
Approved searchable representation
          |
          +----> Keyword / structured search
          |
          +----> Vector similarity
          |
          +----> Knowledge graph relationships
          |

          |
          v

### AI safety and reliability requirements

- Do not send confidential documents to an external AI provider without an approved data-processing and confidentiality arrangement.
- Enforce tenant and deal permissions before retrieving content for model context.
- Treat document text as untrusted data, not as instructions to the AI system.
- Prevent retrieval from leaking confidential records across organizations.
- Store model-generated claims separately from verified facts.
- Use evaluation datasets to measure retrieval precision, ranking quality and hallucination rates.
- Provide an audit record for AI-assisted decisions that materially affect access, review or deal workflows.
- Keep human approval for ownership, legal status, technical certification and financial actions.

## 15.3 Do you need a graph database?

Not at the beginning.

Start with relational entities and explicit relationship tables. Add graph traversal or a dedicated graph database only when real workloads require complex multi-hop queries that are difficult to handle efficiently otherwise.

A technology graph can be a logical domain model long before it becomes a separate graph database.

# 16. Security, privacy, legal and trust architecture

A technology-transfer platform may handle patent strategies, confidential research, trade secrets, source code, unpublished data, contract negotiations and cross-border transactions. Security must be part of the initial architecture.

## 16.1 Security controls

- Strong authentication, with MFA for privileged users and appropriate enterprise controls.
- RBAC/ABAC authorization and resource-level access checks.
- Encryption in transit and at rest.
- Managed secrets and key rotation.
- Private object storage and short-lived access URLs.
- Malware scanning and controlled document processing.
- Rate limiting, abuse prevention and account protection.
- Append-only audit records and monitoring of privileged actions.
- Backups, recovery testing and incident response.
- Dependency scanning, patch management and secure release processes.
- Separation of production, test and development environments.
- Data minimization and explicit retention policies.

## 16.2 Legal and jurisdictional questions to resolve

Before enabling real transactions, establish:

1. Which jurisdictions will the platform operate in?
2. Is it an information marketplace, a broker, a contractual intermediary, or an officially recognized exchange?
3. What evidence is required to authorize an IP transfer or license?
4. Who is responsible for legal review and the accuracy of listings?
5. Which electronic-signature rules apply?
6. Which privacy, data-localization, export-control, sanctions and technology-transfer rules apply?
7. Which payment services require regulated providers?
8. How are disputes, refunds, fraud and misrepresentation handled?
9. How are conflicts of interest disclosed?
10. What legal responsibilities apply to professional service providers?

These questions affect product scope and data flows. They should not be deferred until after the transaction engine has been built.

# 17. Complete development roadmap

I would build this in stages, with explicit exit criteria for each phase. The objective is to establish a useful commercial platform early without painting the system into an architectural corner.

## Phase 0 — Business and domain validation

Before development

Deliverables

- Business model and target market.
- Target jurisdictions and compliance boundaries.
- Stakeholder interviews and user journey maps.
- Domain glossary and entity relationship model.
- Detailed workflow state machines.
- Initial information architecture and UI wireframes.
- Security and confidentiality requirements.
- Monetization experiments and operating cost model.
- Product requirements document (PRD).

Exit criteria: the team can explain how a technology moves from submission to a completed transfer, who is responsible at each stage, and what constitutes a completed deal.

## Phase 1 — Marketplace foundation

Features

- Account registration and authentication.
- Organizations, memberships and permissions.
- Technology registration and versioning.
- Technology publication review.
- Search, filters and technology detail pages.
- Demand registration.
- Basic matching and shortlist management.
- Administration queues and audit logs.
- Private document storage and notifications.

Exit criteria: suppliers can publish reviewed technologies, buyers can submit demands, and both sides can discover and manage qualified opportunities securely.

## Phase 2 — Transfer operations

Features

- Introduction requests.
- NDA workflow and controlled disclosure.
- Restricted deal rooms.
- Broker assignments and expert tasks.
- Due diligence cases.
- Deal pipeline and negotiation records.
- Contract versions and approvals.
- Milestones, reminders and exception handling.

Exit criteria: a qualified opportunity can be managed through diligence and negotiation with a complete history of the process.

## Phase 3 — Commercial transaction infrastructure

Features

- Configurable transaction types.
- Licensing and assignment workflows.
- Contract execution integrations.
- Invoicing and finance reconciliation.
- Fee calculation and revenue reporting.
- Transaction completion evidence.
- Disputes, refunds and audit controls.
- Auction or competitive bidding where appropriate and legally permitted.

Exit criteria: the platform can reliably record and coordinate a transaction, including its financial and contractual conditions, without confusing contract value with platform revenue.

## Phase 4 — Enterprise intelligence and services

Features

- Enterprise subscriptions.
- Managed technology scouting.
- Advanced multilingual search.
- AI-assisted matching and evidence extraction.
- Expert marketplace.
- Technical evaluation and valuation services.
- Enterprise reporting and API access.
- CRM and procurement integrations.

Exit criteria: the platform has repeatable paid services and recurring enterprise usage, rather than depending entirely on sporadic transaction fees.

## Phase 5 — Commercialization network

Features

- Proof-of-concept project management.
- Pilot milestones and validation evidence.
- Manufacturing and industrial partners.
- Financing and investment workflows.
- Regional and international transfer networks.
- Post-transfer outcome tracking.
- Advanced analytics and benchmarking.

Exit criteria: the platform can measure not only transactions but also the deployment and commercial impact of transferred technologies.

### Practical initial scope

I would not attempt all five phases in the first release.

The minimum commercially meaningful product is:

Organization management + technology registry + demand management + discovery and matching + verification + confidential introduction + deal pipeline + administration.

Build the contract, finance and commercialization capabilities on top of that foundation as the first real transactions reveal the necessary requirements.

# 18. Testing and quality assurance

The platform needs more than unit tests for CRUD endpoints.

| Test layer            | What to validate                                                              |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unit tests            | Domain rules, pricing, state transitions, permission decisions                |
| Integration tests     | PostgreSQL, object storage, search, queues and external APIs                  |
| API contract tests    | Request validation, response schemas and error behavior                       |
| Authorization tests   | Cross-tenant access, role escalation and restricted documents                 |
| Workflow tests        | Valid and invalid transitions, timeouts and recovery                          |
| Concurrency tests     | Two users editing a record, duplicate submissions and simultaneous approvals  |
| Security tests        | Upload attacks, IDOR, injection, authentication abuse and data leakage        |
| AI evaluation         | Match relevance, evidence accuracy, multilingual behavior and false positives |
| Financial tests       | Decimal precision, duplicate payments, refunds and reconciliation             |
| Recovery tests        | Database restore, object recovery, event replay and failed integrations       |
| User acceptance tests | Buyer, supplier, broker, expert and administrator scenarios                   |

### Three tests I would treat as release blockers

1. A buyer from Organization A cannot retrieve Organization B's confidential document by guessing its identifier or URL.
2. A failed or repeated payment callback cannot generate duplicate payment records or duplicate accounting effects.
3. A technology suspended for a rights dispute cannot remain discoverable through a stale search index or cached recommendation.

These tests protect the platform's credibility and should be automated.

# 19. Operational dashboards and KPIs

You need separate dashboards for marketplace performance, transfer operations, financial performance and technology quality.

## 19.1 Marketplace metrics

- Verified active technology suppliers.
- Active published technologies.
- Active, qualified demands.
- Percentage of technologies with complete evidence.
- Search-to-inquiry conversion.
- Demand-to-qualified-match conversion.
- Percentage of demands with at least one qualified candidate.
- Repeat buyer and supplier activity.
- Technology category coverage and supply-demand gaps.

## 19.2 Transfer metrics

- Time from technology submission to review.
- Time from demand approval to first qualified match.
- Time from introduction to NDA.
- Time from qualification to contract execution.
- Time from contract execution to completion.
- Deal abandonment by stage and reason.
- Average number of qualified candidates per demand.
- Dispute rate and resolution time.
- Pilot completion and deployment rates.

## 19.3 Financial metrics

- Recognized platform revenue.
- Annual recurring revenue (ARR).
- Revenue by subscription, service and transaction fee.
- Gross margin by revenue stream.
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC).
- Customer lifetime value (LTV).
- Subscription renewal and churn.
- Scouting project margin.
- Invoice collection time.
- Reconciliation exceptions and outstanding liabilities.

## 19.4 AI quality metrics

- Precision among top-ranked matches.
- Recall against expert-identified candidates.
- Percentage of recommendations accepted by buyers.
- Human rejection rate and reasons.
- Match-to-deal conversion.
- Unsupported claim rate in generated summaries.
- Search latency and document-processing failure rate.

Do not optimize only for the number of listed technologies or nominal transaction value. A platform with one million low-quality listings and few qualified matches may be less valuable than a smaller, well-verified marketplace that consistently produces completed transfers.

### Deployment rules

- Keep the primary database private.
- Use managed or controlled secrets; never store credentials in source code.
- Back up both database records and required object-storage content.
- Test recovery rather than relying on successful backup job status.
- Use separate development, staging and production environments.

## My final architectural recommendation

Start with the hybrid model: a demand-driven marketplace, rigorous technology records, controlled verification, explainable matching, and a complete transfer workflow.

Use a modular backend, PostgreSQL as the source of truth, dedicated search, private document storage, explicit state machines, and strong resource-level permissions. Make transaction completion, financial records and legal status separate, auditable concepts.

Most importantly, design the business operations alongside the software. The verification team, technology brokers, deal managers, finance operators and administrators are not secondary users; they are part of the service that makes the marketplace trustworthy.

Once the platform profile above is settled, the next engineering artifact should be a complete implementation specification: detailed database DDL and indexes, module and package structure, API request/response schemas, role-permission matrix, page-by-page UI behavior, state-transition rules, event contracts, infrastructure configuration, test cases, and a dependency-ordered development backlog. That is the level of detail needed to move from this blueprint into implementation without guessing at the business logic.
