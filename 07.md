# Technology exchange platform: real-world roles, workflows, approvals and money movement

You're right to challenge the earlier design. A technology exchange cannot be designed properly by inventing a few user roles and CRUD screens. We need to derive its workflows from how real technology-transfer organizations and marketplaces actually operate, then decide which mechanisms belong in your platform.

I researched real examples from the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), the Enterprise Europe Network (EEN), university technology-transfer offices, and the commercial technology-scouting and licensing firm yet2. They demonstrate an important distinction:

A technology exchange is not just a marketplace where someone posts a technology and someone else buys it. It may coordinate identity and organization verification, discovery, confidential disclosure, technical evaluation, intellectual-property due diligence, negotiation, contracting, payment milestones, delivery, acceptance, and dispute resolution.

Real platforms do not all handle the same parts. WIPO GREEN, for example, connects technology providers and seekers and supports licensing, collaboration, joint ventures and sales; EEN facilitates partner searches and introductions; university technology-transfer offices handle invention assessment and licensing; yet2 provides hands-on scouting and deal facilitation. These are complementary operating models, not interchangeable templates.&#x20;

## 2. The user groups your platform actually needs

Do not model everyone as just “buyer” or “seller.” A single organization can be a supplier in one deal and a buyer in another. Within that organization, different people need different authority.

I recommend five layers: participants, organizational roles, platform operations, professional advisers, and payment/compliance services.

### A. Core participants

| User group                            | Who they are                                               | What they do                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Technology provider                   | Company, startup, inventor, research institution           | Publishes technology, supplies evidence, answers questions, negotiates rights and commercial terms        |
| Technology seeker                     | Company, manufacturer, public agency, research institution | Describes a technical problem, searches, evaluates candidates, negotiates and acquires rights or services |
| R&D collaborator                      | Company, university, lab, research consortium              | Proposes joint research, contributes staff/data/equipment/funding, agrees ownership of new results        |
| Investor or commercialization partner | Investor, corporate venture team, accelerator              | Evaluates opportunities and may finance development or commercialization                                  |
| Technology intermediary               | Technology-transfer office, broker, innovation consultant  | Represents a party, finds partners, supports introductions and negotiations                               |

These categories reflect real-world technology-offer/request databases and licensing workflows. EEN distinguishes technology and business offers/requests and R&D requests; WIPO GREEN supports technology providers, seekers and experts; technology-transfer offices manage IP commercialization.&#x20;

### B. Organization-level roles

Every participating organization should support multiple members with different permissions.

| Role                               | Typical permissions                                            | Must not automatically be allowed to do                              |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Organization owner                 | Manage organization profile, membership, organization settings | Sign every contract merely because they created the account          |
| Technology/IP owner representative | Submit technologies, confirm rights ownership or authority     | Claim rights belonging to another institution without authorization  |
| Technology manager                 | Edit listings, respond to enquiries, upload technical evidence | Commit the organization to a binding deal unless authorized          |
| Buyer/project manager              | Create demands, shortlist technologies, coordinate evaluation  | Approve funding or execute a contract without the relevant authority |
| Technical evaluator                | Review specifications, conduct tests, submit reports           | See unrelated confidential deals or authorize payments               |
| Legal/IP reviewer                  | Review NDA, IP ownership, licensing scope and contracts        | Release funds unless separately authorized                           |
| Finance approver                   | Approve budgets, invoices, payments and refunds                | Change technical acceptance criteria unilaterally                    |
| Contract signatory                 | Execute agreements within their delegated authority            | Sign for another organization                                        |
| Read-only auditor                  | Inspect permitted deal records and financial history           | Edit, approve or delete records                                      |

Key implementation rule: account ownership, organizational authority, and deal approval are separate concepts. The person who submits a technology may not own the IP, and the person who discusses a price may not have authority to commit the organization.

### C. Platform operations and external specialists

| Group                       | Responsibility                                                                             |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Platform administrator      | Platform configuration, user support, access management                                    |
| Verification/review officer | Reviews organization evidence, listing completeness and identity checks                    |
| Exchange case manager       | Coordinates introductions, meetings, deadlines and follow-ups                              |
| Technical expert            | Independent assessment, testing, validation or feasibility report                          |
| IP/legal adviser            | Supports rights checks, licensing terms and agreement review                               |
| Payment provider            | Processes payments, transfers and payouts where supported                                  |
| Dispute reviewer/mediator   | Handles escalated disputes under a defined process                                         |
| Compliance officer          | Handles sanctions, fraud, conflicts, regulated activities and escalations where applicable |

For a small initial team, one staff member can wear several of these hats. However, the permissions and audit records should remain distinct, particularly for approval of payments, changing bank details, releasing funds and resolving disputes.

You do not need to build a large expert network on day one. You do need to design the platform so external experts can be invited into specific cases without gaining broad access to confidential information.

## 3. The business workflows for each group

The workflows below are the recommended product design, informed by the real processes above. They are not a claim that every reference organization follows this exact sequence.

### Workflow A — Technology provider

1\. Register and establish authority

Submit contact details, organization details, jurisdiction, representative information and any requested proof of authority.

2\. Submit a technology listing

Describe the solution, problem solved, development stage, evidence, IP status, available rights, intended applications and preferred partnership types.

3\. Review and publish

Correct incomplete information, respond to reviewer questions, and approve the public-facing listing.

4\. Receive and qualify enquiries

Accept, decline or request clarification. Confirm that the interested party and intended use are credible.

5\. Confidential evaluation and negotiation

Agree confidentiality terms if needed, share authorized technical information, answer questions and review proposals.

6\. Execute the deal

Approve the final agreement through an authorized signatory, complete any funding conditions, and deliver the agreed technology or services.

7\. Confirm performance and receive payment

Submit milestone evidence or invoices, respond to acceptance feedback, receive released payments and handle continuing obligations such as royalties.

This workflow follows the logic of technology disclosure, evaluation, partner identification and licensing described by university technology-transfer offices.&#x20;

What the provider should submit

- Public technology summary and intended applications.
- Technical specifications, maturity level and supporting evidence.
- IP information: patent numbers or applications where relevant, ownership, inventors/creators, co-owners and existing encumbrances.
- Authority to offer the technology, if the submitter is not the owner.
- Commercialization preferences: license, assignment, joint development, supply, technical services or another collaboration.
- Proposed commercial terms, which may be indicative rather than binding.
- Confidential documents only through access-controlled disclosure.

Do not force every provider to submit a patent number. Some valuable technologies are protected by copyright, know-how, trade secrets or contractual rights. But the platform must distinguish “IP claimed by submitter” from “IP independently verified.” WIPO specifically cautions users not to disclose trade secrets in public database submissions.&#x20;

### Workflow B — Technology seeker / buyer

1. Register the organization and users. Establish the identity of the organization and who is authorized to represent it.

2. Submit a technology demand. State the problem, required performance, deployment environment, budget range, deadline, preferred collaboration type and acceptance criteria.

3. Review candidate technologies. Search and shortlist candidates; request further information or ask the platform to find alternatives.

4. Submit an expression of interest. Explain the use case, desired partnership, capability to evaluate or commercialize, and information needed. This is consistent with the fields in EEN's real partnership enquiry form.&#x20;

   [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://een.ec.europa.eu\&sz=32)

   Enterprise Europe Network

   +1

5. Confirm the evaluation plan. Agree what documents, samples, demonstrations, tests and success criteria will be used.

6. Conduct technical and commercial due diligence. Evaluate technical fit, rights, implementation cost, supplier capability and financial feasibility.

7. Negotiate and approve the deal. Agree scope, rights, fees, milestones, acceptance tests, liability and termination provisions.

8. Fund the agreed obligations. Pay the applicable deposit or milestone amount through the agreed payment route.

9. Evaluate delivery and confirm acceptance. Accept, reject with evidence, or request permitted corrections.

10. Complete payment and ongoing obligations. Release payment when the contract's conditions are satisfied, then manage support, royalties, renewals and compliance if applicable.

A buyer's “accept” action must have a clear meaning. Accepting an enquiry, accepting a technical result, accepting a contract and accepting a delivered milestone are four different actions. The system should never treat them as interchangeable.

### Workflow C — R&D collaborators and investors

These users need a slightly different deal model because the outcome may be a jointly developed technology rather than the delivery of an existing product.

They should be able to submit or approve:

- Project proposal, work packages, budget and timeline.
- Contributions of staff, facilities, equipment, data and cash.
- Background IP each party brings into the project.
- Ownership and licensing rules for newly developed results.
- Publication, confidentiality, patent filing and commercialization rules.
- Funding commitments, drawdown conditions and milestone evidence.
- Decisions to continue, pause, change scope or terminate.

WIPO identifies R&D cooperation agreements, licensing agreements, assignments and confidentiality agreements as distinct technology-transfer instruments. That is a strong reason to make the deal type a first-class field instead of forcing all transactions into one generic purchase workflow.&#x20;

### Workflow D — Platform case manager

Your managed-exchange staff need an operational workflow of their own:

1. Review the enquiry for completeness and suitability.
2. Confirm that the relevant parties can be introduced.
3. Obtain consent before sharing identities or non-public information.
4. Coordinate NDA execution and controlled document access where needed.
5. Arrange technical calls, demonstrations, samples or expert evaluations.
6. Record unresolved questions, commitments, meeting outcomes and deadlines.
7. Escalate IP, legal, financial or compliance issues to the appropriate specialist.
8. Track each party's position on commercial terms.
9. Coordinate execution of the final agreement.
10. Track milestones, disputes and completion, without unilaterally overriding either party's contractual rights.

This is particularly relevant to the managed model described by yet2, which includes introductions, NDAs, samples and evaluations, and to EEN's model of advisers supporting partner search and follow-up.&#x20;

## 4. What does “submit, accept, confirm, discuss and agree” mean?

These words sound similar in ordinary conversation but must have distinct meanings in the system.

| Action                  | Meaning                                                                      | What the platform should record                                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Submit                  | A user sends a new or revised item                                           | Version, submitter, timestamp and required fields                  |
| Review                  | A reviewer checks the item against defined criteria                          | Reviewer, decision, evidence and reason                            |
| Approve for publication | Platform permits a listing to be publicly discoverable                       | Approval scope, reviewer and publication version                   |
| Express interest        | A party asks to explore an opportunity                                       | Use case, requested information and intended collaboration         |
| Accept an introduction  | A party consents to contact or a managed introduction                        | Consent, scope and visibility permissions                          |
| Accept an NDA           | A party executes or otherwise validly accepts the confidentiality instrument | Document version, parties, signature evidence and effective status |
| Discuss                 | Parties exchange messages, questions and proposals                           | Participants, timestamps, attachments and confidentiality level    |
| Propose terms           | A party submits a commercial or technical proposal                           | Immutable proposal version, expiry and terms                       |
| Counteroffer            | A party changes one or more proposed terms                                   | New version linked to the prior version                            |
| Agree terms             | Parties confirm the same complete set of terms                               | Exact version accepted by each authorized party                    |
| Sign contract           | Authorized representatives execute the agreement                             | Executed contract and signature/verification evidence              |
| Fund                    | Required money is received and confirmed by the payment provider or bank     | Payment reference, amount, currency and confirmed status           |
| Deliver                 | A party submits the agreed output or evidence                                | Delivery version, timestamp and relevant files                     |
| Accept milestone        | The designated party confirms contractual acceptance                         | Criteria, decision, evidence and authorized actor                  |
| Release payment         | The payment route transfers the approved amount                              | Provider reference, amount, fees, recipient and status             |
| Withdraw funds          | A recipient requests a payout of an eligible balance                         | Amount, payout destination, verification and provider result       |
| Dispute                 | A party contests a payment, delivery, acceptance or other obligation         | Disputed issue, evidence, deadlines and resolution history         |

The crucial design principle is that an action must create an auditable event, not just change a text field.

### Consensus is not the same as agreement

For a two-party licensing deal, there is usually no need for a generic “consensus” button. What you need is a versioned term sheet and explicit acceptance by every required party.

For example:

- Provider proposes a license fee of €40,000.
- Buyer counters with €25,000 plus a milestone payment.
- Provider accepts the new amount but changes the license territory.
- Buyer must review the changed territory; its earlier acceptance of the price is not acceptance of the new complete proposal.
- Both parties approve the same final version.
- Authorized signatories execute the definitive contract.

For an R&D consortium, the same principle applies but the required approvals may involve three or more organizations and multiple work packages.

WIPO's licensing guidance emphasizes that terms are negotiated for the specific circumstances, with confidentiality, rights, scope, payment and other obligations addressed in the actual agreement.&#x20;

## 5. Confidentiality, discussion and due diligence

This part is essential for a technology exchange. A buyer needs enough information to decide whether to proceed, but a provider may not be willing to disclose source code, manufacturing details, unpublished inventions or trade secrets to an unqualified stranger.

A sensible disclosure ladder is:

Level 1 — Public listing

Non-confidential summary, applications, maturity, publicly disclosed IP and non-sensitive evidence.

Level 2 — Qualified enquiry

More detailed questions, business fit, buyer use case, organization capability and non-sensitive supporting information.

Level 3 — Confidential evaluation

Appropriate NDA or confidentiality arrangement, restricted documents, demonstrations, samples or technical data.

Level 4 — Contract and implementation

Contractual deliverables, authorized technical handover, detailed implementation material and records of accepted milestones.

This staged approach is supported by WIPO's guidance on NDAs and trade secrets. Its guidance recommends defining the confidential information, authorized use, permitted recipients, exceptions and what happens to confidential materials if negotiations end. An NDA should not be treated as a license or as automatic permission to use the technology.&#x20;

The platform should therefore support:

- Document-specific access grants, rather than access to an entire deal folder.
- Time-limited and revocable access where legally and technically appropriate.
- A log of who viewed or downloaded each confidential file.
- NDA versions and a clear record of which parties are covered.
- An explicit decision about whether the parties may retain or must return/destroy confidential material when talks end.
- A rule that privileged information is not exposed in public search, automated matching or notifications.

The platform can record that a party claims to have destroyed a file, but it cannot honestly guarantee deletion of every copy a recipient may have downloaded.

## 6. The financial lifecycle: quote, fund, lock, release, withdraw

This is the most important architectural distinction in your question.

A technology marketplace should not invent an “escrow balance” merely because it has a database. A recorded obligation, money received by a payment provider, money reserved for a transaction and money available to withdraw are different things.

WIPO's guidance establishes that technology-license payment terms can include upfront fees, milestone payments, royalties and other negotiated compensation. Rice University and the University of Minnesota likewise describe upfront, milestone, patent-cost reimbursement, royalties and minimum-royalty structures in their licensing processes.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.wipo.int\&sz=32)

### A. The recommended payment lifecycle

1\. Commercial terms agreed

The parties agree price, currency, due date, conditions, refund rules and which event makes each payment payable. No money has necessarily moved.

2\. Payment requested

The platform creates a payment obligation and a payment request. The buyer knows the amount, beneficiary and purpose.

3\. Funds received and confirmed

The payment provider confirms the relevant payment status. A browser redirect or buyer screenshot is not sufficient proof by itself.

4\. Funds reserved or held, if supported

If the payment product and legal arrangement support the intended holding structure, the funds remain subject to the specified conditions. Otherwise, record the contractual status without pretending that the platform controls the money.

5\. Milestone evidence submitted

The provider submits the agreed deliverable or evidence. The buyer reviews it against previously agreed criteria.

6\. Acceptance or dispute

The authorized buyer accepts the milestone, requests a correction permitted by contract, or opens a dispute with a reason and supporting evidence.

7\. Payment released or otherwise settled

The authorized payment instruction is sent. The platform waits for the provider's confirmed result and reconciles the payment and any platform fee.

8\. Payout to beneficiary

The payment provider or bank sends the eligible proceeds to the recipient's verified payout destination. Payout pending, paid, failed and reversed are distinct states.

This is a proposed workflow, not a universal escrow process. The available payment mechanism depends on your jurisdiction, provider, deal type and contract.

### B. What exactly should “locked” mean?

Do not use one status called `locked` for every situation. At least four meanings are possible:

| Term                                       | Meaning                                                                         | Where the state belongs                  |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Contractually committed                    | The buyer owes a payment if the agreed conditions are met                       | Contract / obligation                    |
| Payment initiated                          | The payment instruction has been created but is not yet confirmed as successful | Payment record                           |
| Provider balance pending                   | Funds are in a payment-provider balance and not yet available for payout        | Provider balance / payout status         |
| Funds reserved under a holding arrangement | Money is restricted under a supported legal and payment structure               | Holding arrangement and provider records |

The platform should only say “funds secured” if the actual payment arrangement justifies that statement. A buyer clicking “Fund deal” is not proof that the funds have been received, and a successful charge is not necessarily the same as a completed payout.

For example, Stripe Connect documents separate charges and transfers, allowing a platform to collect a payment and later transfer portions to connected accounts. Its documentation also describes processing fees, refunds, chargebacks, transfer timing and transfer reversals. This is a payment-routing mechanism, not proof that every such arrangement constitutes legally protected escrow.&#x20;

### C. What happens when a buyer pays?

Suppose the contract calls for a €20,000 upfront license payment, with a separate €10,000 milestone payment later.

The system should not assume the whole €30,000 is immediately payable or releasable.

1. The contract creates two obligations: €20,000 upfront and €10,000 upon the specified milestone.
2. The first payment request is issued.
3. The payment provider confirms the first payment.
4. The system records the confirmed amount, payment reference, currency and relevant provider fees.
5. If the agreed payment setup allows the funds to be reserved pending a condition, the system records that provider-backed status.
6. The provider delivers the milestone specified by the contract.
7. The buyer accepts it, or disputes it under the agreed process.
8. The platform requests the corresponding release/transfer where supported.
9. The provider reports the result; the platform reconciles the records.
10. The second payment is requested only when its contractual trigger is satisfied.

The numbers are illustrative, not a standard market tariff. The key is that every payment is linked to an obligation, and every release is linked to an explicit reason and authorization.

### D. What if the buyer does not accept the deliverable?

The platform needs a predetermined procedure, not an administrator who can simply choose a winner.

The contract or milestone plan should specify:

- Objective acceptance criteria.
- Who may test and accept the deliverable.
- A review deadline.
- What evidence must accompany a rejection.
- Whether a correction or retest is permitted.
- What happens if the buyer does not respond.
- How a dispute is escalated.
- What happens to the associated payment while a dispute is pending.
- What remedies are available under the contract.

Do not implement automatic acceptance after a deadline unless the parties explicitly agreed to that mechanism and it is legally appropriate for the deal. The platform should not invent a rule that changes the parties' contractual rights.

### E. Fees: what should the platform charge for?

There is no single universal technology-transfer marketplace fee. Your business model must decide this.

I recommend configuring fee rules by deal type, rather than hardcoding a single commission across all transactions.

| Possible fee              | When charged                                                                 | Recommended initial treatment                                |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Registration              | Account or organization onboarding                                           | Free initially, unless there is a clear reason to charge     |
| Listing/publication       | Technology accepted for publication                                          | Free initially to encourage supply                           |
| Managed-service fee       | Platform staff perform search, diligence coordination or negotiation support | Fixed fee, staged fee or separately quoted service           |
| Success fee               | A qualifying transaction completes                                           | Percentage or fixed amount, clearly defined in the agreement |
| Payment processing fee    | Payment provider charges for a transaction                                   | Show separately; specify who bears it                        |
| Expert assessment fee     | Technical or IP specialist performs paid work                                | Quote and approve before commissioning                       |
| Contract/document service | Optional professional service                                                | Clearly priced and separately accepted                       |
| Renewal or monitoring fee | Continuing service after a deal                                              | Introduce only if the ongoing value is clear                 |

These are design options, not claims that the cited organizations all charge these fees. In particular, EEN provides its partnering database and advice as part of its network service, while commercial service providers such as yet2 offer paid scouting and licensing services. Different operating models have different cost structures.&#x20;

For each fee, record:

- Who owes it.
- Who receives it.
- What event makes it payable.
- The amount or calculation formula.
- Currency, tax treatment and whether the fee is refundable.
- Whether it is deducted from a payment or invoiced separately.
- What happens if the deal is cancelled or the payment is refunded.

Do not deduct a success fee merely because a buyer clicked “Accept introduction.” Define the qualifying event in advance, such as execution of a license agreement, completion of a specified transaction or receipt of a defined payment. The appropriate event depends on your business model and contract.

### F. Withdrawal and payout

A recipient should be able to request a payout only from funds that the payment system actually makes available for payout.

A reasonable payout flow is:

1. The recipient completes any required identity, organization and payout-account verification.
2. The system shows confirmed available proceeds, pending proceeds and amounts subject to restrictions separately.
3. The recipient submits a withdrawal request.
4. The platform validates the amount, payout destination, transaction restrictions and any applicable reserve or dispute hold.
5. The payment provider processes the payout.
6. The platform records the provider's result: pending, paid, failed, returned or reversed.
7. A failed payout remains visible and is investigated rather than being silently marked complete.

A withdrawal request is not a withdrawal, just as a transfer instruction is not proof that the recipient received the money.

If your initial platform launches in Ireland or elsewhere in the European Economic Area, payment-services rules require careful analysis. The Central Bank of Ireland's guidance describes safeguarding obligations for relevant payment institutions, including segregation of users' funds where applicable. Do not assume that calling an internal database balance “escrow” avoids those requirements. Obtain jurisdiction-specific legal advice before holding or controlling customer funds.&#x20;

## 7. Different deal types require different payment and acceptance rules

A technology exchange should support multiple deal types because the deliverable and the economic model are different.

| Deal type                     | Typical deliverable                                               | Typical payment model                                        | What confirms completion?                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| IP license                    | Contractual permission to use specified IP                        | Upfront fee, milestones, royalties or a combination          | Effective license and satisfaction of relevant contractual conditions |
| IP assignment                 | Ownership transfer of specified IP                                | Lump sum or staged payments                                  | Required transfer documents and any required formalities              |
| Technical services            | Engineering, integration, consulting or implementation            | Deposit and milestone/final payments                         | Defined services and acceptance criteria                              |
| R&D collaboration             | Research work, prototypes, test results or jointly developed IP   | Funding tranches, milestone payments or agreed contributions | Work-package evidence and contractually defined review                |
| Technology supply             | Product, equipment, material, software or know-how package        | Purchase payment, delivery milestones or agreed terms        | Delivery and applicable acceptance tests                              |
| Commercialization partnership | Manufacturing, distribution, market access or sublicensing rights | Fixed fees, royalties, revenue share or mixed terms          | Contractual launch, sales or reporting obligations                    |
| Investment                    | Equity or another negotiated investment instrument                | Funding round, tranche or closing payment                    | Completion of investment conditions and required documents            |

The payment examples above are common design patterns, not guaranteed terms for every transaction. WIPO explicitly notes that technology-transfer contracts vary by circumstances and that licenses, assignments, confidentiality agreements and R&D cooperation agreements serve different purposes. ## 9. What the first working version should contain

For a small team, I would not build a complete payment institution, a universal escrow service, a complex e-signature system and an expert network at the same time.

Build this vertical slice first:

1. Organization onboarding: registration, membership, roles and basic verification.
2. Technology and demand listings: structured submissions, reviewer workflow, publication and version history.
3. Introductions: expressions of interest, acceptance/decline, managed introductions and communication records.
4. Confidential evaluation: NDA status, controlled document sharing and due-diligence checklist.
5. Negotiation: versioned term sheet, counteroffers, approvals and explicit agreement on the final version.
6. Contract and milestones: signed agreement, defined deliverables, acceptance criteria, deadlines and evidence.
7. Financial integration: invoices/payment requests, provider-confirmed payments, fee calculation, payout status, refunds and reconciliation.
8. Dispute and audit: escalation, evidence preservation, permission-controlled review and a complete event history.

In the first release, the platform can coordinate an invoice and record that a buyer paid a supplier directly, if that is the chosen arrangement. That is much simpler than receiving, safeguarding, reserving and distributing money yourself. You can later introduce provider-backed delayed transfers or a legally reviewed holding structure if your market actually needs them.
